# Copy

Copy sounds machine-written when it was written before anyone gathered what is true about this client. Swapping words does not cure it; collecting facts and making choices does.

## 1. The fact sheet, before any sentence

Write it in the contract's notes or your working file, never on the page. One line each, from the spec, the user or the project, in their words where you can:

- **The business and its customer:** a sentence a neighbour would say ("a bakery by the station that bakes to order"), then who would be better served elsewhere.
- **The alternative** the customer uses now, and what this client does that it does not, with the evidence.
- **The offer:** the one action, its terms, prices if given.
- **Practical facts:** place, hours, contacts, dates.
- **Specifics that are true and a little particular:** how something is made, a house habit, a limit the client states (only by appointment, no parking).

Leave a line blank when nothing fills it. The page then carries less on that point, and the handoff asks the client for it.

## 2. Nothing invented

- No metric, testimonial, rating, client logo, case count, award, price, date, founding year or team member that the fact sheet does not hold.
- A missing fact the visitor needs to act becomes a visible gap in the page's muted style ("Horário a confirmar"), at most two per page, each listed in the handoff. The rest are left out, not announced.
- No placeholder text (lorem ipsum, "your text here", square brackets, TODO) and no stock people or brands.
- Every link goes somewhere real: a section of this page or a URL given. Never `href="#"`. When the real URL is unknown, link to the part of this page closest to what the visitor wanted, and the handoff asks for the URL.
- Without an endpoint, a form still checks its fields, then tells the visitor plainly that nothing was sent. It never shows a success message.

## 3. The budget, per page

A device used everywhere stops working anywhere. Per page:

- **One** headline built from two short fragments ("Two chairs. No rush.").
- **Two** contrasts in total from the "X, not Y" family, counting "rather than" and "instead of" as members: they stand in for each other.
- **None** of the words that praise the client's own character (trusted, passionate, dedicated, human): show the fact that would earn the word.

## 4. Actions and headings

- The primary action has one wording, used in the nav, the first screen and the close: an action verb and its object ("Reservar mesa", "Order the cake"). Vague labels (Learn more, Click here) leave the visitor guessing.
- A section heading tells the visitor what they will find there (prices, the menu, how to get here). Generic labels such as "About us" or "Our services" say nothing, and riddles say less.
- A label above a heading stays only when it carries a fact, a destination or an identifier the reader would otherwise lack (a date, a filename, a step of a real sequence). Cover it: if the section still tells the reader everything, the label was decoration and comes out.
- Error messages name the problem and the way out; an empty list explains what will appear there and how to add the first item.

## 5. Checks

- **Swap test:** would the main heading, the sub-line and each section heading stay true for a competitor, or the same trade in another city? Then they say nothing about this client; rebuild them from the fact sheet.
- **The owner's mouth:** keep only sentences the client would say about their own business to a customer in person.
- **Second pass:** a fixed tic tends to grow a new one (every sentence in pairs, contrasts multiplying); read once more for it.
- Text in the page's language, with its punctuation and accents; the document's `lang` matches it.
